# Reading filed accounts

The figures are already extracted. This is about what to do with them: which
comparisons carry information, which look meaningful and do not, and where a
standard ratio is the wrong question for the industry in front of you.

Every diagnostic below is written to be computable from an income statement, a
balance sheet and a cash flow statement. Where one needs a figure that may not
be there, that is said, because the absence is itself worth reporting.

## The order to read in

1. **Does revenue grow, and is the growth real?** Revenue against the same
   period a year earlier, never against a different-length period. Then against
   receivables: revenue that arrives as an unpaid invoice is a weaker fact than
   revenue that arrives as cash.
2. **Does growth reach profit?** Gross margin, operating margin, net margin, in
   that order. A rising gross margin with a falling operating margin says costs
   are growing below the gross line; the reverse says pricing or input costs
   moved.
3. **Does profit become cash?** Operating cash flow against net income. This is
   the single most informative check in the accounts.
4. **What does keeping it running cost?** Capital spending against revenue and
   against operating cash flow.
5. **Could it survive a bad year?** Current ratio, debt against equity, cash
   against debt, and the worst year visible in the history shown.

## Diagnostics worth running

**Cash conversion.** Operating cash flow ÷ net income. Near or above 1.0 is
healthy. A ratio falling across periods while revenue rises is the classic
signature of growth funded by working capital: the sales are booked, the cash
has not arrived. Quote the ratio for each period shown rather than the latest
alone; the trend is the finding.

**The accrual check.** (Net income − operating cash flow) ÷ total assets. A
large positive figure means reported profit is running ahead of cash generation.
It is not evidence of wrongdoing; it is a reason to ask which line is absorbing
the difference, and receivables and inventory are the usual answers.

**Days of receivables.** Receivables ÷ revenue for the period × days in the
period. Compare to the same period a year earlier, not to a general rule: what
is normal differs by industry. Rising days against flat revenue means collection
is slowing; rising days with surging revenue may simply be timing.

**Days of inventory.** Inventory ÷ cost of sales for the period × days in the
period. Cost of sales is revenue less gross profit where it is not given
directly. Inventory rising much faster than sales is where a demand
disappointment shows up first, as write-downs.

**Free cash flow and its margin.** Operating cash flow − capital spending, and
that over revenue. Then ask where it went: dividends, buybacks, acquisitions,
debt repayment. If those are not in the table, say the destination is unknown
rather than assuming it was retained.

**Capital intensity.** Capital spending ÷ revenue, and capital spending ÷
operating cash flow. The second matters more in a downturn: spending commitments
do not fall as fast as revenue can, which is how a profitable business reaches a
negative free cash flow year.

**Return on equity, carefully.** Net income ÷ shareholders' equity. Two traps:
an income figure for one period divided by a balance from another date is not a
return, so say which dates you used; and equity that has swollen from retained
profit will depress the ratio precisely when the business is earning most.

**DuPont decomposition.** Return on equity = net margin × asset turnover
(revenue ÷ assets) × leverage (assets ÷ equity). Worth doing when return on
equity moves and the reason is not obvious: it separates a margin story from a
turnover story from a borrowing story.

**Dilution.** Share count across the periods shown, and share-based pay against
net income. A rising count means per-share figures flatter the owner less than
the totals suggest. Per-share history is unreliable across a stock split unless
the figures have been restated.

**The worst year on the page.** Find the weakest period in the history shown and
quote it. In a cyclical business this is the most useful single fact in the
accounts, and it is the one a strong current year hides.

## Where the standard ratios mislead

**Banks and insurers.** Gross margin is meaningless; revenue is interest and fee
income, and "cost of sales" does not exist. Read net interest income, loan-loss
provisions, and the loan book against deposits. Leverage is the business model,
not a warning sign.

**Property and REITs.** Depreciation dominates reported earnings, so price to
earnings misleads. Funds from operations and the debt maturity schedule are the
figures that matter, and neither is a standard XBRL line.

**Software and subscriptions.** Deferred revenue (a contract liability) is cash
already collected for service not yet delivered: rising deferred revenue is a
leading indicator of revenue, and falling deferred revenue is a warning the
income statement will not show for several quarters.

**Semiconductors, memory, shipping, energy, mining.** Violently cyclical. A
peak-year multiple computed on peak earnings is the single most misleading
number it is possible to produce from these accounts. Always set the current
margin against the trough year in the history shown.

**Pharmaceuticals and biotechnology.** R&D is the business; expensing it makes a
successful developer look unprofitable. Patent expiry and pipeline are not in
the accounts, and their absence should be stated.

**Retail and restaurants.** Total revenue growth hides whether existing shops
are growing or the company is simply opening more. Same-store sales are rarely
in XBRL; say so.

**Holding companies and conglomerates.** Consolidated figures blend businesses
with different economics. Without the segment note, margins are an average of
things that should not be averaged.

## Multiples, where a price is available

Compute on the last twelve months, not on the last completed year, wherever an
interim filing allows the roll-forward: last full year, minus the prior-year
interim, plus the current interim. For a company mid-cycle the difference is the
difference between a useful figure and a meaningless one.

Never compute a multiple across currencies. A price in one currency over
earnings filed in another is arithmetic without meaning, and a depositary share
may represent several ordinary shares at a ratio not given in the filings.

A multiple with no peer group and no history of its own cannot be called cheap
or expensive. State it, explain what it represents, and say what would be needed
to judge it.

## A growth investor's checklist: CAN SLIM

CAN SLIM is William O'Neil's checklist for growing companies. Use it as a set
of questions to keep in mind while you read, not as a scorecard to fill in.
Each letter names what it asks, what in the facts answers it, and where a
good answer misleads.

**C, current earnings.** Is profit per share in the latest quarter well up on
the same quarter a year earlier, and are sales up too? O'Neil looked for
around 25% or more, and for growth that is speeding up. The quarter table
answers it. It misleads when the year-earlier quarter was very weak, so the
growth is a rebound. It misleads too when the rise comes from a one-off gain,
a lower tax rate or buybacks rather than from the business.

**A, annual earnings.** Has profit per share grown steadily over three to
five years, with a good return on equity (O'Neil looked for 17% or more)?
The yearly table answers it. In a cyclical business (memory, shipping,
energy, mining, steel) three strong years can be the top of a cycle. The best
growth often comes just before the fall, so set it against the worst year on
the page.

**N, something new.** Is there a new product, a new market, new management
or a new way of selling that could carry growth from here? The company's
release, its plans and the news answer it. A new product is only a story
until it shows in sales or orders, so say whether it has. A share at a new
high is momentum, not proof of value.

**S, supply and demand for the shares.** Is the share count falling or
rising, and is volume heavy on the days the price rises? The share count,
the dilution check and the volume figures answer it. Buybacks paid for with
borrowing, or made at any price, flatter profit per share without growing
the business.

**L, leader or laggard.** Has the share done better than the market and its
industry over the past year? Is the company the leader in what it does? The
moves set beside the S&P 500's, and the company's place among its rivals,
answer it. A leader in a crowded theme may already be priced for everything
going right.

**I, institutional sponsorship.** Are more funds buying than selling, and
are they good ones? The fund figures from Nasdaq answer it. Very heavy fund
ownership cuts both ways: there are few buyers left, and many sellers if the
story turns.

**M, market direction.** Is the market as a whole rising? Most shares follow
it. The backdrop answers it. It says nothing about the company itself, so
use it only as context.

**Where the checklist does not fit.** It is built for growing companies that
already make a profit. It says little about banks and insurers, property
companies, utilities, or drug developers without sales. For those, say the
checklist does not apply and lean on the sector notes above.

## What the accounts cannot tell you

Say these plainly rather than reaching past them: customer concentration unless
disclosed; order book and guidance; competitive position; management quality;
what the price already reflects; anything that happened after the balance sheet
date. The age of the latest filing is part of the analysis, not a footnote.
